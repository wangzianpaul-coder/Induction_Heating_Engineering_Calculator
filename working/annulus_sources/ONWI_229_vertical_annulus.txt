ABSTRACT
Heat transfer correlations are developed for a  vertical annul us 
wherein the inner cylinder is e le c tric a lly  heated at a  rate scaled 
to that expected in a  typical pressurized or boiling water reactor 
subassembly. The outer cylinder is maintained at a  constant temper­
ature and provisions have been m ad e  in the design of the apparatus for 
future experiments with multiple rod arrays having a  specific power 
generation s im ila rily  scaled to that in a  fuel rod subassembly. Over­
a ll heat transfer data are corrected for thermal radiation in the 
annul us; and Rayleigh numbers, based on the gap width, span the 
conduction, transition and turbulent regimes of free convection. A  
total heat transfer coefficient is also obtained for each run via 
linearization of the radiative transport across the annulus.
Heat transfer coefficients are obtained with a ir and helium as 
the f i l l  media. The range or Rayleigh numbers for the present datag
is 300 <  R a  <  2.3 X  10 .  Generally, the energy transferred by radi­
ation across the annulus, as a  fraction of total heat transfer, varies 
with Rayleigh number and f i l l  medium. With a ir as the f i l l  medium, 
thermal radiation can account for more than 4 0 %  of the heat transfer. 
With helium, radiation can account fo r up to one-third of the heat 
transfer rate.
The results of the present work establish a  baseline of compari­
son of heat transfer coefficients to be obtained with a  multiple rod 
array and for the assessment of the effects of thermal radiation on 
heat transfer in a  fuel rod bundle.

SECTION 1. INTRODUCTION
Heat transfer within a  spent fuel canister is an area where 
engineering correlations are not presently available to the extent 
required for the design and analysis of a  spent fuel repository. This 
is due, in part, to the complex internal geometry of the fuel rod 
assembly and to the variety of thermal boundary conditions that m ay 
exist on the outer surface of the canister and in the sleeve between the 
canister and the host rock of the repository. In the development of 
waste acceptance c rite ria  for spent fuel and in the design of a  spent 
fuel repository, the m axim um  temperature within the canister m ay have to 
be specified. In addition, the m axim um  surface temperature of the 
canister or the temperature gradient within the near-field region of the 
repository room  m ay also be specified as part of the waste acceptance 
c rite ria  [5,20]*. The setting of these temperature lim its w ill require 
detailed knowledge of the convective and the combined convective and 
radiative heat transfer processes within the canister and fuel rod 
assembly. The heat transfer processes w ill, in turn depend on internal 
canister geometry, specific thermal load ( i.e ., burnup and age of the 
fuel at emplacement) and possibly, the type of f i l l  m edium  within the 
canister, e.g., gas or otherwise. Related issues concern temperature 
effects on the corrosion rate of the canister during the period of 
re trie v a b ility  and the thermal loading per unit area of the repository. 
Whatever thermal constraints are set on the design on the repository and 
the waste package, a n  important engineering factor w ill be the heat 
transfer to the canister from the heat-generating fuel rods.
From  a  fundamental point of view, the problem of free convection in 
a  vertical cylinder with discrete heat sources, i.e ., rods, forms the 
basis fo r the computation of heat transfer rates to the surrounding

walls and near-field of the repository. Both local and average heat 
transfer coefficients are of interest, as are temperature distributions 
as a  function of axial location on the rods. In general, heat transfer 
coefficients in free convection w ill depend on the overall 
height-to-diameter ra tio  of the cylinder, specific and average power 
generation of the rods, the thermal boundary conditions on the canister 
surface, and the type of gas or f i l l  m edium  within the canister. Thus, 
correlations for the Nusselt number are expected to involve the Grashof 
number, the Prandtl number, an overall aspect ratio of the cylinder, a  
rod spacing parameter, and the number of rods and th e ir configuration.
For a ll but the simplest cases, i.e ., the vertical annulus, flow 
fields are strongly three dimensional, and determining the heat transfer 
relation requires either an experimental approach or a  very costly 
numerical approach. W h e n  effects of thermal radiation between 
individual rods and between the rods and the canister wall are 
superimposed upon free convection, the heat transfer problem is m ade a ll 
the more complex via the introduction of nonlinear thermal boundary 
conditions and surface properties which m ay be a  function of position 
and time within the canister.
At present, there appears to be only a  few studies which treat free 
convection in fuel rod bundles that m ay have general ap p lica b ility to 
spent fuel disposal. Thermal radiation within fuel rod bundles has been 
studied both analytically and experimentally in connection with failu re 
of subassembly canisters and cladding in a  loss of coolant accident. 
Taken together, these works point to the need for experimental data and 
analyses of heat transfer for completely enclosed fuel rod bundles, or 
subassemblies, over an extended range of Grashof numbers and radiation 
parameters. Additionally, work is also needed on simpler geometries, 
e.g., the vertical annulus or a  cylinder with a  few heated rods to 
establish fundamental, or 1 imiting-case, heat transfer correlations.
In th is report, w e  describe laboratory experiments on heat transfer 
in a  vertical annulus from the conduction to the turbulent regimes of 
free convection. Radiation between the walls of the annulus is


generally included in the measurements of the overall heat transfer 
coefficient at the inner cylinder. Heat transfer coefficients for free 
convection alone are obtained by applying a  correction for the radiative 
component of transfer obtained by measurements at low pressure. The 
specific heat generation rate of the inner cylinder is scaled to that 
expected in a  typical pressurized or boiling water reactor fuel rod 
bundle.

Llewellyn [18] has m ade a  preliminary comparison of the transient 
temperature distributions experienced in the storage of ten year old 
spent fuel assemblies (and high-level waste) in salt repositories with 
areal power density of 150 kW/acre and 3.5 kW/canister. Two-dimensional

that the decay rate of spent fuel always produces a  larger average

areal thermal loading. For example, after forty years in the 
repository, a  peak temperature of 337°F was obtained for spent fuel 
compared to 301°F in high-level waste. In these calculations, heat 
transfer processes within the waste packages were not considered, and 
thus peak temperatures represent volumetrically averaged values.
Recently, Benjamin et al. [1 ] reported the results of a  study of 
spent fuel heat up following a  loss of water during pool storage. 
Calculations were m ade taking into account the effects of decay time, 
fuel element design, storage rack design, packing density, drainage

fuel. They find that clad rupture, or melting, following complete loss 
of water is strongly dependent on the storage configuration and the 
decay period. Furthermore, they conclude that the minimum prerequisite 
storage period to preclude clad fa ilu re  can vary from less than ten days
for som e storage configurations to years for others. N o  consideration 
was given to the disposal problem wherein heat transfer mechanisms are 
less effective due to the absence of a  liquid cooling medium. However, 
th e ir study points to the detail one must consider in the heat transfer 
calculations when predictions of individual rod temperatures are needed.

SECTION 3. DESIGN O F THE EXPERIMENT
3.1 Introduction
Heat transfer in spent fuel disposal involves the coupled processes 
of free convection and thermal radiation inside the canister and 
combined convection and conduction at the waste package-repository 
interface. The internal problem is complicated by the close spacing of 
the heat generating fuel rods, which leads to a  non-uniform 
distributions of heat transfer coefficients and radiosity on each rod 
within the bundle.
A s mentioned in Section 1, the lim iting case problem is that of 
heat transfer in a  vertical annulus, for which so m e  data are available. 
O ne step removed from this is the determination of heat transfer 
coefficients in a  bundle of several heated rods in a  vertical cylinder. 
In this section, w e  describe the design and construction of a  laboratory 
apparatus with which to conduct experiments on both of these problems. 
The apparatus constructed for the present study is nearly a  "thermally" 
equivalent scale model of a  spent fuel assembly and canister.

accidental and systematic errors. The la tte r results because of

dimensionless numbers.

the result.
